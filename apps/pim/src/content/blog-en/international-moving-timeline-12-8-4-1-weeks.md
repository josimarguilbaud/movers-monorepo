Almost no problem in an international move shows up on loading day. It shows up weeks earlier, in the shape of a decision that got postponed. The passport that expired, the destination document that took a month, the vehicle that could not be shipped without a prior formality.

The fix is not working faster at the end. It is **working in the right order from the start**. Here is the milestone timeline for an international move, based on the process we actually follow: survey and advice, professional packing, transit and customs, final delivery.

The intervals are a planning reference. If your destination requires slow immigration formalities, start earlier.

## 1. Twelve weeks out: decide and measure

This is the definition phase. Nothing gets packed here; everything gets decided.

- **Request the volume survey.** In person or by video call. It is what turns an idea into a number: your real cubic meters, or **CBM**, the unit ocean freight is quoted in. Without that number there is no serious quote, only estimates that change later.
- **Choose the route.** Ocean in an exclusive container, shared ocean, or air. Each has a different cost and time profile. Our [international sea freight moving](/en/services/international-sea-freight-moving/) page explains when each mode makes sense.
- **Review your personal documents.** Passports valid with room to spare, visas, residence permits. Many countries condition the household goods tax exemption on a specific immigration status, so this point is logistical, not just personal.
- **Confirm special requirements.** Pets and vehicles have their own calendars, and they are long. If an animal is coming, the veterinary file starts here, not in week 4.
- **Read up on your corridor.** Every country has its own household goods regime and its own timings. If your destination is Spain — the most asked about from Panama — start with [moving from Panama to Spain](/en/destinations/moving-from-panama-to-spain/).
- **Start the purge.** Every object you decide to take occupies volume, and volume gets paid for. Twelve weeks is enough to sell, donate, and discard without rushing.

## 2. Eight weeks out: close decisions and contracts

With the quote in hand, this phase turns plans into commitments.

- **Book the service and set the loading date.** The sailing date depends on carrier availability; the earlier it is fixed, the more options there are.
- **Decide where each thing ends up.** What goes in the container, what travels with you in your luggage, what goes into temporary storage, and what gets sold. Writing it down saves redoing it.
- **Declare the value of your goods for insurance.** Be precise here. **Do not inflate the value** to "be extra safe": you pay an unnecessary premium. **Do not understate it** to save: if something happens, the settlement will not cover replacing it.
- **Gather your customs documentation.** Passport, destination immigration document, valued inventory. The exact list depends on the corridor and your coordinator provides it.
- **Settle appliances and electronics.** If your destination runs on a different voltage or frequency, decide now what goes and what gets sold. An appliance that is useless at destination pays freight for nothing.
- **Notify schools, banks, and utilities.** School records and medical histories take time to issue.

## 3. Four weeks out: prepare the house

Now the move becomes physical.

- **Use up or dispose of anything that cannot travel.** Food, liquids, aerosols, flammables, paints, cleaning products. Plants and seeds do not pass either. Your coordinator gives you the exact prohibited list for your destination.
- **Set aside the "first weeks kit."** Clothing for the destination climate, medication, chargers, documents, some basic household items. That kit **does not go in the container**: it travels with you or by air.
- **Separate the irreplaceable documents.** Passports, birth and marriage certificates, diplomas, medical records, property deeds. They go in your carry-on. Always.
- **Prepare the appliances.** Defrost and dry the refrigerator well in advance; drain the washer and dishwasher. Equipment with residual water grows mold inside a container.
- **Confirm access at origin and destination.** Elevators, building management permits, time restrictions, street width for the truck. A container that cannot park costs time and money.
- **Photograph anything valuable.** Documented prior condition, in case a claim is needed.

## 4. One week out: the last loose ends

- **Reconfirm the date, time, and loading crew** with your coordinator.
- **Empty drawers and dismantle what is yours to dismantle.** The professional packing crew takes apart anything needing tools; you empty the personal contents.
- **Close accounts at origin:** electricity, water, internet, alarms. Schedule the disconnections for after loading day, not before: you will need the lights.
- **Do the final inventory review.** Everything loaded must match what is declared. A discrepancy between a box and the declaration is a customs problem, not a detail.
- **Check destination requirements again.** Regulations change. This is the last useful window to react.

## 5. Loading days: professional packing

The crew arrives, packs, and stows. What you need to do that day is little, but it matters:

- **Be present, or leave someone with authority to decide.**
- **Point out the fragile and high-value items** before they are packed, not after.
- **Check the labeling.** Every piece is marked by contents and room: that label is what organizes the delivery later.
- **Sign the inventory when it is complete**, not before.
- **Walk the empty house** with the supervisor before the truck leaves.

The **seal** — the numbered tag that closes the container and evidences it was not opened in transit — goes on last. Write down its number.

## 6. Transit and customs: what happens without you

Here begins the part you do not see. The container goes to the port, is loaded, sails, and arrives at destination, where a local agent files the documentation for **customs clearance**. If it traveled in a shared container, there is also a **deconsolidation**: separating your household goods from the other shipments that shared the container.

On timing, it pays to be honest: **total time combines the ocean transit with the origin and destination processes (packing, customs, and delivery). We confirm the exact estimate for your corridor in your quote.** Nobody can give you an exact date to the day months ahead, because there are variables — weather, port congestion, customs inspection — that do not depend on the carrier. What you can demand is to be told when they change.

## 7. Final delivery

The truck arrives at your new address. At this point:

- **Check the seal** before it is opened.
- **Check the inventory** piece by piece as it is unloaded.
- **Direct where each item goes**, using the room labeling.
- **Document any damage immediately**, with photos and in writing, before signing off. Claiming after signing without remarks is far harder.

## In short

A well-run international move is a timeline, not a sprint. Twelve weeks out you measure and decide; eight weeks out you book and document; four weeks out you prepare the house; one week out you close loose ends. On loading day you only execute what was already settled. And when something slips, it is almost always a destination formality requested too late.
