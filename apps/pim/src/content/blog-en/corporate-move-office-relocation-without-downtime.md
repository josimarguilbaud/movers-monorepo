In a residential move, the worst case is spending one night among boxes. In a corporate move it has an hourly cost: staff who cannot work, clients with no answer, processes at a standstill.

That is why an office move is not managed like a large household move, but like a **project**: with phases, owners, and a continuity plan. Here is how it is structured.

## 1. The goal is not moving boxes: it is reducing downtime

Before counting a single piece of furniture, define the metric. In a corporate relocation what you optimize is **downtime**: the working hours during which a workstation cannot operate.

That metric changes every decision. It explains why the work happens at night or over a weekend. It explains why the move is phased instead of done all at once. And it explains why so much goes into labeling: not for tidiness, but because **reassembly costs more than disassembly**. Taking an office apart is fast; putting it back together in the right place is where the days get lost.

Said plainly: **downtime does not reach zero**. Anyone promising you an office move with no interruption at all is describing something that does not exist. What you can do is concentrate it outside productive hours and reduce it to the strict minimum.

## 2. Advance assessment: the floor plan rules

The project starts with two documents, and neither one is the quote.

- **Inventory by area.** Furniture, equipment, archives, stockroom materials, and special items, counted and classified by department.
- **Floor plan of the new site with workstation assignments.** Who sits where, which piece of furniture goes in each position, where the meeting rooms, reception, archive, and comms room end up.

That plan is what governs the labeling. Without it there is no corporate move: there is a temporary warehouse in your new office.

Add three data points people tend to forget: **the electrical and network capacity of each position** at destination, **the dimensions of doors, corridors, and elevators** in both buildings, and **the furniture that will not be used again**. That last one is money straight out the door: moving a filing cabinet you are going to discard is paying to discard it twice.

## 3. Phased planning: the heart of the method

Moving an entire office in a single push maximizes risk. Working in phases spreads it out.

The sequences that work best:

1. **Phase zero: what interrupts nothing.** Dead archives, stockroom, surplus furniture, rooms not used daily. This moves while the office operates normally, during business hours.
2. **Infrastructure phase.** Cabling, network, and the destination comms room ready and tested **before** a single desk arrives. This is the classic mistake: moving equipment to a site where the network is not yet working.
3. **Department by department.** One full area moves per window — typically a weekend or a night — while the rest keeps operating. The relocated area resumes work at the new site the next day.
4. **Closing phase.** Reception, meeting rooms, shared furniture, and the final clear-out at origin.

Companies with customer-facing shifts often invert the order: a minimum team that can operate from the new site moves first, and only once it is confirmed to be responding does the rest follow.

## 4. Labeling by workstation

This is where Monday's productivity gets decided. The system allows no exceptions: **every item carries the code of its destination workstation, not its origin**. A typical code combines floor, area, and workstation number, and that same code goes on the floor plan and on the physical position at destination.

Practical rules that prevent chaos:

- **Color by department**, code by workstation. Color is visible from a distance and organizes the unload; the code organizes the fine placement.
- **Each employee packs their own personal contents** in boxes labeled with their code. Nobody knows their drawer better than they do.
- **Each workstation's cables travel with that workstation**, in a labeled bag, not in a shared "cables" box. A shared cable box is a lost afternoon.
- **Furniture gets labeled too**, not just boxes. A desk with no code ends up in the hallway.
- **Anything with no assigned destination does not go on the truck.** That gets decided beforehand: discarded, donated, or sent to storage.

## 5. IT equipment: the most delicate asset

Computer equipment concentrates both the highest unit value and the highest operational risk. It gets handled separately.

- **Data backup is the client's responsibility and happens beforehand.** No moving company is accountable for data. Verify complete, tested backups before disconnecting anything.
- **Servers and network equipment** require specialized packing and, where applicable, coordination with your IT provider for an orderly shutdown and startup.
- **Monitors, CPUs, and peripherals** travel individually protected, not loosely stacked. The original box is ideal where it exists.
- **Photograph the back of every unit before disconnecting.** Reconnecting without that photo is guesswork.
- **Schedule reconnection and testing** as a project milestone, with an assigned time, not as something that "happens at the end."

## 6. Physical archives and confidential documents

The archive is the material that most quietly falls out of order and the one that causes the most legal trouble if lost.

- **Preserve the internal order.** Boxes are filled respecting the sequence of the files; series do not get mixed.
- **Number sequentially** and record each box's range in the inventory. A box with no declared range is a box someone will have to open.
- **Chain of custody for confidential material**, with a record of who hands over and who receives.
- **Purge before moving.** Moving archives that have passed their retention period is paying freight on paper you are about to destroy.

If you need to free up space at the new site, low-consultation archives can go to [storage](/en/services/storage-solutions-panama/) instead of taking up expensive square meters.

## 7. Coordinating with both building managements

This point is always underestimated, and it is the one that most often stops a loaded truck.

Before setting the date, confirm in writing at **both** buildings:

- **Permitted hours** for moves and use of the loading dock.
- **Service elevator booking**, and its real dimensions and capacity.
- **Mandatory protection** of floors, walls, and elevators.
- **Personnel access requirements:** name lists, IDs, and any insurance or certificates the management demands of the provider.
- **Parking space and permit** for the truck, and municipal traffic restrictions where applicable.

A corporate building may allow moves only outside office hours. That is not an obstacle: it is a data point that, known in time, defines the schedule. Known late, it destroys it.

## 8. When the site changes country

If the relocation crosses a border, two layers are added to the project. The first is customs: office furniture and equipment are **commercial cargo**, not household goods, and they are declared and taxed as such. The second is human: the management team relocating also moves their homes, on a calendar of their own tied to immigration status.

These are best treated as two coordinated projects rather than one. The company's operation gets planned in phases; each family's move gets planned corridor by corridor — for example, at [moving from Panama to the United States](/en/destinations/moving-from-panama-to-united-states/) — with the support of [executive relocation](/en/services/executive-expat-relocation/).

## 9. The day after

The move does not end with the last truck. Build into the plan an **on-site support window** for the first business day to fix workstations that will not start, a **closing walkthrough** with outstanding items by area, and the **formal handover of the origin premises**, including the final cleaning if the lease requires it.

## In short

An office move is measured in hours of downtime, not in boxes. You win it with a workstation floor plan drawn up before the quote, a phased relocation that has the network ready before the desks, labeling by destination workstation with no exceptions, separate handling for IT equipment and confidential archives, and both building managements confirmed in writing before the date is set.
