Spain is the most asked-about destination for people leaving Panama: the language, the historical ties, an established community, and its position as a gateway to Europe. Madrid, Barcelona, and Valencia account for most relocations, but the corridor serves any Spanish city.

What follows is not a list of reasons to move. It is the arc of the operation: what you take, what Spanish customs requires of household goods, what to settle before shipping, and what happens on arrival.

## 1. Volume first, everything else after

Every international move starts the same way: **how many cubic meters your home takes up**. **CBM** — the cubic meter, the unit ocean freight is quoted in — determines the mode, the cost, and much of the calendar. It is not estimated over the phone: it comes from a **volume survey**, in person or by video call, free and with no obligation.

## 2. Choosing the route: exclusive ocean, shared ocean, or air

For Spain there are three routes, and volume almost always decides.

- **Exclusive container (FCL).** Your household goods travel alone in a sealed container. The usual route for a full house: less handling in transit and a more direct calendar.
- **Shared container (LCL, or groupage).** You share the container with other shipments and pay only for the space you occupy. Worth it when you would not fill a 20-foot container. In exchange, at destination there is a **deconsolidation** — separating your shipment from the others — which adds a step.
- **Air freight.** For small volumes or urgency: your "first weeks kit," or whatever you need before the ship arrives.

Many families combine the two: the bulk by sea and one air shipment with the essentials. Compare the modes at [international sea freight moving](/en/services/international-sea-freight-moving/).

On timing, let's be precise: **total time combines the ocean transit with the origin and destination processes (packing, customs, and delivery). We confirm the exact estimate for your corridor in your quote.** Be wary of anyone promising a date to the day months in advance: there are port and customs variables no carrier controls.

## 3. What is worth the freight and what is not

Every object that goes into the container takes up volume, and volume gets paid for. Sort your things into four groups:

1. **Irreplaceable.** Documents, photos, heirlooms, artwork. These always go. The small and valuable ones travel with you, not in the container.
2. **Quality furniture.** Worth it if replacing it in Spain costs more than moving it. Consider size too: Spanish homes tend to be more compact, and a dining set designed for a large house may not fit in an urban flat.
3. **Cheap and bulky.** Budget furniture, old furnishings, consumables. They take up expensive space and are replaced for little.
4. **Things you no longer use.** Donate, sell, or discard before the survey, not after: it reduces the volume you get quoted on.

**Appliances** deserve their own chapter. Panama runs on 110V and 60 Hz, with type A and B plugs; Spain runs on 230V and 50 Hz, with type C and F plugs. The plug, the voltage, and the frequency all change. Motor-driven appliances — washer, refrigerator, dryer — rarely justify the freight. Modern electronics whose power supply reads `100-240V ~ 50/60Hz` only need a plug adapter.

And what **cannot travel** under any circumstances: flammables, aerosols, paints, explosives, corrosives, perishable food, plants, seeds and plant material, weapons and ammunition.

## 4. What Spanish customs asks of household goods

This is the point that raises the most questions and demands the most honesty.

Spain provides for **relief on the import of used household goods when transferring residence**, subject to specific conditions and documentation. The logic is the one shared by almost every European country: the relief exists for someone **genuinely transferring their residence**, not for someone importing merchandise.

In practice, that translates into requirements of this kind:

- **Proving the transfer of residence** to Spain, with the immigration documentation that matches your situation.
- **Showing the goods are used** and for personal or household use, not new and not destined for sale.
- **Presenting a valued inventory**, consistent with what is actually in the container.
- **Respecting time limits** between the change of residence and the import, and **undertakings not to dispose of the goods** for a subsequent period.

**We are not going to give you figures or specific deadlines in an article.** The thresholds, time windows, and exact documents are set by the customs regulations in force, they change, and they depend on your immigration situation. Any number published today may be out of date by the time you ship; your case gets reviewed when we quote, with the current rules in front of us.

What is worth remembering is the operational consequence: **your immigration file governs the moving calendar**. If the document proving your residence does not exist yet, shipping early can cost you the relief. That sequence gets planned, not discovered at the port.

## 5. The inventory: your most important document

In a move to Spain, the inventory does three jobs at once: it is the **basis of the customs declaration** — what arrives has to match what was declared — it is the **backing for the insurance**, and it is the **guide for final delivery**, because labeling by contents and room is what organizes the unload.

On valuation, the rule is the usual one: **do not inflate the value** to "be extra safe," because you pay an unnecessary premium; **do not understate it** to save, because if something happens the settlement will not cover replacing it.

## 6. What to settle before shipping

Clear these before the loading date, not after:

- **Personal documents in hand.** Passports, Spanish immigration paperwork, birth and marriage certificates, diplomas and academic records, medical histories. **None of this goes in the container**: it travels in your carry-on.
- **A delivery address in Spain, or a storage plan**, if you have not closed on a home yet.
- **Access at destination.** Older Spanish buildings have narrow stairs, tight entrances, and small elevators. Confirm measurements and municipal parking permits before the truck leaves.
- **First weeks kit.** Clothing for your city's climate — the humid north and the cold-winter interior are nothing like Panama — medication, chargers, and basic household items.
- **Pets and vehicles, on their own calendars.** Their files are independent and longer than the household goods file.

## 7. Vehicles and pets: two separate projects

**A vehicle** is not just another box. Importing a car into the European Union involves technical, type-approval, and tax requirements different from household goods, and the arithmetic often does not favor bringing it. It gets assessed case by case at [car shipping in a container](/en/services/car-shipping-in-container/).

**A pet** has its own sequence, and the order is mandatory: a standard microchip, a rabies vaccine given after the chip, a serology test where the destination requires it, a health certificate within its validity window, and entry through a designated point. The waiting periods force you to start months ahead.

## 8. What happens on arrival

The container reaches the Spanish port, a local agent files the documentation, and **customs clearance** is processed. If it traveled shared, deconsolidation comes first. Then inland transport to your address, and delivery.

There are three things to do that day, in this order: **check the seal** — the numbered tag proving the container was not opened in transit — before it is opened, **check the inventory** piece by piece as it is unloaded, and **document any damage in writing and with photos before signing off**. Claiming after you have signed without remarks is far harder.

## In short

Moving to Spain from Panama comes down to four decisions: how much volume you move, by which route, what immigration documentation proves your change of residence to Spanish customs, and what you leave behind because it does not justify the freight — starting with motor-driven appliances, which run on 110V and 60 Hz here and will not there. Customs relief for used household goods exists, but it depends on conditions that change: it gets reviewed with your case in front of us, not from a generic list.
